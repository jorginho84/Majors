# Meeting minutes — 2026-11-17

**Attendees:** not stated in the notes (Emilio, Jorge, José Ignacio, Agustín and Andrés are mentioned).

## Topics discussed
- **Design V balance table (issue #10, `47_design5_balance_noctrl.do`):**
  - Coefficients are in percentage points per ten extra seats. Without controls, 100 extra seats predict 1 pp *fewer* private-subsidized students.
  - Program and year FE (column 3) cut the coefficients substantially. The private-subsidized coefficient falls from ~1 to ~0.35.
  - With PSU language + math controls, predicted graduation is still significant: +0.038 pp per ten seats (+0.38 pp per 100). The effect is small but not zero.
  - Overall: balance is not perfect, but the controls do a lot of work. The core problem is that the balance table cannot show triple stars. We need a variable that explains the residual rise in predicted graduation, and then control for it in every specification.
- **ROL fixed effects, same-sample test:**
  - The coefficient roughly halves between columns 1 and 4. But columns 1–3 and 4–6 differ in two ways at once: the FE specification and the sample (full sample vs. the 84k non-singleton sample).
  - Proposed diagnostic: re-estimate columns 1–3 on the 84k non-singleton sample.
    - If the coefficient drops from ~0.7 to ~0.04 on the same sample, the exact ROL FE are absorbing graduation-relevant information about how students rank programs.
    - If it stays similar, the gap is driven by sample composition, not by the FE choice.
  - Also suggested: try every ROL variant to see whether any drives predicted graduation to zero. This was already partly explored.
- **University-level expenditure regressions (`45_finance_seats_rf.do`):**
  - Specification: growth in a university outcome on seat growth in T, T−1 and T−2, controlling for the change in field mix, with year FE, 2013–2022.
  - These are correlations, not IV estimates: the outcomes are at the university level while seat variation is at the program level.
  - Results for a 10% rise in seats in T:
    - First-year students +4.9%; undergraduate students +2.3%.
    - Total expenses +0.7% (ns); capital unchanged.
    - Expenses per student −1.6%; capital per student −3%.
    - Tuition revenue keeps growing in T−1 and T−2.
  - Interpretation: programs that expand are not spending more per student at the same time. At the university level, this rules out a spending channel behind higher graduation.
  - Andrés raised a possible confound: a shift in composition toward fee-paying vs. scholarship students.
  - Emilio flagged that the first-difference specification constrains the coefficient on lagged Y to 1. Proposed alternative (a suggestion, not yet a firm plan): levels with university and year FE.
    - Start with a column with seats in T only (the unconditional correlation), then add T−1 and T−2 one at a time.
    - Log-log (elasticities) is fine.
  - Tentative proposal: add expenditure per student as a control in the balance specification, since the balance regressions have no university × year FE. Caveat: it is a university-level variable, so it may not be granular enough to drive predicted graduation to zero.
- **Value added with Central Bank earnings (issues #9, #11):**
  - Agustín discussed the VA regressions with Emilio over the weekend.
  - Program-level estimates cannot be extracted under the bank-data disclosure rules. Results cannot rest on fewer than 25 observations of any unit (students, universities, legal entities), and fewer than 10 is very hard to clear.
  - Workaround: quartile-level summaries by program. These likely meet the threshold and are enough to tell whether expanding programs tend to have high or low VA. Target: next week.
- **Ministry of Labor:** the contact was emailed last week and again today. No reply yet.

## Decisions

- Use quartile-level program VA from Central Bank data instead of program-level estimates.
