\begin{frame}{Overview: what we are trying to do}
\small
\textbf{Motivating question.} Why don't we increase capacity in high-return programs?

\vspace{0.3cm}
\textbf{Step 0.} We first identify \emph{which} programs have high returns, with a program value-added estimated from students admitted to the same set of programs (first section).

\vspace{0.3cm}
Expanding a program's capacity has two sides, and we study each one:
\begin{itemize}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{6pt}
    \item \textbf{At the margin (RDD).} What happens if we change capacity at the margin?
    Applicants just above the admission cutoff get the seat, and those just below do not.
    Comparing them tells us what an additional seat does for the student who receives it.
    \item \textbf{Inframarginal students.} What happens to the students who would have been admitted anyway
    if we change capacity? A larger program may change class size and who their classmates are, so more seats could affect students already in the program.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.3cm}
Together, the two pieces tell us whether expanding capacity is worthwhile: the gain for the marginal student, and what happens to everyone else in the program.
\end{frame}

%--------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Overview: the five inframarginal designs}
\small
\begin{description}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{6pt}
    \item[I. Yearly vacancy changes] Every year-to-year change in a program's vacancies, used as an instrument for the size of its entering cohort (2SLS).
    \item[II. Own sudden expansion] A selective program suddenly increases its seats. Difference-in-differences with an event study.
    \item[III. Entry of new universities (SUA)] Programs more exposed to the 2012 entry of new universities, compared with less exposed ones.
    \item[IV. Close competitors' sudden expansions] A program is treated once a close competitor (same market, entrant PSU within 50 points) suddenly increases its seats. Difference-in-differences with an event study.
    \item[V. Design I at the individual level] The same yearly vacancy changes, but with one row per inframarginal student, individual controls and fixed effects for the student's application portfolio (ROL). 2SLS.
\end{description}

\vspace{0.3cm}
In every design we look at three things: \textbf{enrollment} (does the program get more or fewer students?),
\textbf{composition} (does the type of entrant change?) and \textbf{graduation of the inframarginal students}.
\end{frame}

%--------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Main results: changing capacity at the margin (RDD)}
\small
An additional seat for applicants at the cutoff (crossing the admission cutoff):
\begin{itemize}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{5pt}
    \item \textbf{Target-program enrollment}: $+50.7$ pp.
    \item \textbf{Target-program graduation (8 years)}: $+18.0$ pp.
    \item Graduation from \emph{any university}: $+2.9$ pp. From \emph{any higher-education institution}: $+2.4$ pp.
    \item PSU retaking: $-4.9$ pp.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.3cm}
\textbf{Reading.} The seat changes \emph{where} the student enrolls and graduates, much more than \emph{whether} they do:
most rejected applicants find another program. The effect is present in every field and is larger when the target
program is much more selective than the next-best option.
\end{frame}

%--------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Main results: changing capacity, effects on inframarginal students}
\footnotesize
\begin{description}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{6pt}
    \item[I.] The first stage is strong (about 0.74 more entrants per added seat). The effect on inframarginal graduation is zero with year FE and small within university and year:
    $-0.33$ pp per 10 additional entrants (mean 36.9\%). Graduation from any university does not fall, so students switch programs rather than drop out.
    \item[II.] A sudden expansion adds about 23 entrants and lowers entrant PSU by 5 to 9 points. The negative effect on inframarginal graduation
    comes from a decline that was already under way before the expansion, and disappears with university $\times$ year FE ($-1.5$ pp, not significant).
    \item[III.] The first stage has the opposite sign to the one predicted, rejects the pre-trend test and is no longer significant once region $\times$ year FE are added. The reduced form on inframarginal graduation is negative, but the event study shows no break in 2012.
    \item[IV.] When a close competitor expands, the program's enrollment \emph{rises} by about 3 students and its entrants are weaker (NEM $-4$, PSU $-2$ to $-5$), with flat pre-trends.
    Inframarginal graduation at 8 years does not change. Among selective programs, program graduation falls by 4 pp with program and year FE, but not with field $\times$ year FE or CS.
    \item[V.] Design I at the student level (432,184 students). The first stage is strong and portfolio FE do not move the estimate. Program graduation \emph{rises} by 0.06 to 0.09 pp per entrant, the sign of Design I in levels (not in first differences). Balance fails slightly and next year's vacancies predict this year's graduation, so the variation is not clean: program trends load on vacancies.
\end{description}
\end{frame}

%--------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Main take-away}
\normalsize
\begin{enumerate}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{8pt}
    \item \textbf{At the margin}, an additional seat decides which program the student attends, not whether they study.
    It raises graduation from the target program, with larger effects when the target program is much more selective than the next-best alternative.
    \item \textbf{Inframarginal students} are barely affected when capacity changes. The cleanest estimate (Design I) is small
    and points to switching programs, not dropping out.
    \item The larger effects in Designs II--IV \textbf{do not survive} event studies or richer fixed effects. Design V shows that the sign of Design I depends on whether program trends are removed (levels vs.\ first differences).
    \item So the evidence does not show a large cost of expanding capacity for the students already in the program.
    The question of \emph{why capacity is not expanded in high-return programs} is therefore not answered by costs to inframarginal students.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\section{Step 0: Which programs have high returns? Program value-added}

\begin{frame}{Why this step}
\small
\textbf{The question of the paper.} What happens if \emph{high-return} programs expand their vacancies?
\begin{itemize}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{4pt}
    \item For the \textbf{marginal} applicant who gets the extra seat (RDD at the cutoff).
    \item For the \textbf{inframarginal} students who would have been admitted anyway (Designs I--V).
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.2cm}
\textbf{This step is an input, not a main result.} To ask that question we first need to know \emph{which} programs have high returns.
Raw outcome comparisons do not tell us: PSU, NEM and family background sort students across programs.

\vspace{0.2cm}
\textbf{What we do.} We estimate a value-added (VA) for every program and use its distribution to classify programs into high- and low-return.
We adapt Mountjoy \& Hickman (2021, ``The Returns to College(s)'') to the Chilean centralized admission system.
\end{frame}

%--------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Identification: same admission set, different program}
\small
\textbf{Mountjoy \& Hickman (Texas).} Compare students who were \emph{admitted to the same set of colleges} but enrolled in different ones.

\vspace{0.2cm}
\textbf{Adaptation to DEMRE's deferred acceptance.} A student is assigned to the top-ranked program in her ROL where she is \emph{feasible} (score $\geq$ cutoff).
\begin{itemize}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{4pt}
    \item \textbf{Portfolio} $A_i$ = cohort $\times$ the \emph{unordered} set of feasible ROL programs.
    \item \textbf{Treatment variation} = the \emph{order} of the ROL within that set. Two students feasible at $\{j,k\}$ end up in $j$ or $k$ only because of how they ranked them.
    \item Crossing a cutoff changes $A_i$, so the RD variation is absorbed: VA uses variation that is disjoint from the RD.
\end{itemize}
\[
Y_i=\sum_j v_j\,\mathbf{1}[S_i=j]+\phi_{A_i}+g(\text{PSU},\text{NEM},\text{rank})+X_i'\beta+\varepsilon_i
\]
\scriptsize
\textbf{Key assumption:} within a portfolio, the ROL order is unrelated to potential outcomes. $S_i$: program assigned by DEMRE.
$v_j$ is relative to the enrollment-weighted mean. Robust SE; signal SD $=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\hat v)-\overline{SE^2}}$; Empirical Bayes shrinkage.
\end{frame}

%--------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Data and variables}
\small
\begin{itemize}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{4pt}
    \item \textbf{Applications} (Formulario C), cohorts 2007--2016: full ROL, status, scores. Program weights and cutoffs.
    We recompute the weighted score for \emph{every} ROL entry (DEMRE's score is 0 below the assigned preference).
    \item \textbf{Outcome: income rank.} Percentile (1--100), among graduates, of the Mi Futuro income of the program of the student's
    first title within 8 years (switchers included). \textbf{0 if no title.}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Mi Futuro: mean income in the 4th year after the title, institution $\times$ \emph{carrera gen\'erica}, reference years 2017--2024.
        \item Placeholder until individual wages are available. Secondary outcome: any title within 8 years.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Controls:} cubics in PSU (L--M), NEM and ranking; sex; school dependency.
    \item \textbf{Program characteristics:} entrants' PSU, NEM, school type and sex; field (SIES); regular vacancies 2007--2025 (Oferta Acad\'emica).
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.1cm}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Caveat.} At the program level the outcome partly embeds the program's own (raw) Mi Futuro income, so VA here measures value-added on the
\emph{expected earnings of the credential}. Individual wages will remove this.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Step 0: take-aways}
\small
\begin{enumerate}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{5pt}
    \item The DEMRE assignment makes the Mountjoy--Hickman design feasible: 77\% of assigned students have two or more feasible programs, and the ROL order generates within-portfolio variation.
    \item Within portfolios, observable selection disappears (balance), and controls no longer matter.
    \item VA varies substantially across programs (signal SD 8.9 rank points), much less than raw outcomes, and is only weakly related to selectivity. Field accounts for a third of it, the institution for a quarter.
    \item After 2012, the top VA decile expanded its vacancies clearly more than the rest (+17 log points by 2025, against $-7$ to $+9$ for deciles 1--9).
    \item \textbf{Next:} validate VA against the RD at each cutoff (forecast-bias test). Then use the VA classification to study what happens to marginal and inframarginal students when high-return programs expand.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\section{RDD: Threshold crossing effects}

\begin{frame}{RDD around admission cutoffs}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Running variable: application score centered around the regular admission cutoff,
    \[
    r_{ipt}=score_{ipt}-cutoff_{pt}.
    \]

    \item Treatment indicator:
    \[
    A_{ipt}=1(r_{ipt}\geq 0).
    \]

    \item We compare applicants marginally above and below the cutoff within the same program-year.

    \item Main specification:
\end{itemize}

\vspace{-0.2cm}

\[
Y_{ipt}
=
\beta A_{ipt}
+
f(r_{ipt})
+
\mu_{pt}
+
\varepsilon_{ipt}
\]

\vspace{0.15cm}

\footnotesize
Main bandwidth: $|r_{ipt}| \leq 25$. Standard errors are clustered at the program-year level.

\end{frame}

\subsection{Inframarginal Design No. 1}
%====================================================
% DESIGN I: VACANCY CHANGES AND INFRAMARGINAL GRADUATION (issue #5)
% code/04_rdd/Inframarginals/31_design1_main.do (main, placebo, robustness)
%====================================================
\begin{frame}{Design I: the question and the three ingredients}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Question.} When a program admits a larger entering cohort, do the students who would have been admitted
anyway (the \emph{inframarginal} students) become less likely to graduate?

\vspace{0.12cm}
\textbf{Why inframarginals.} The RDD above identifies effects for applicants \emph{at} the admission cutoff, a very particular group.
Inframarginal students are ranked well inside the admitted group. A small change in capacity does not decide whether they get in;
it only changes \emph{who their classmates are and how crowded the program is}.
Two mechanisms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Congestion}: larger classes, fewer resources per student.
    \item \textbf{Peer composition}: expansions admit weaker entrants (see the composition slide).
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.12cm}
\textbf{The exercise has three ingredients, defined one by one on the next slides:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Who} is inframarginal in program $p$: applicants whose admission rank is at most a fixed threshold $T_p$.
    \item \textbf{Treatment and instrument}: the size of the entering cohort $N_{pt}$, instrumented by the vacancies offered $Z_{pt}$.
    \item \textbf{Outcome}: the share of those inframarginal applicants who graduate within 8 years.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

%--------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Step 1. Who is inframarginal?}
\scriptsize
\textbf{Admission rank.} $Rank_{ipt}$ is the position of applicant $i$ in the selection list of program $p$ in year $t$
(DEMRE \texttt{LUGAR}). It exists only for applicants who were admitted or wait-listed, so only they can be inframarginal.

\vspace{0.08cm}
\textbf{Threshold.} Each program gets \emph{one} number $T_p$, the same for all years 2007--2016, built from its
\emph{enrolled} cohort sizes $N_{pt}$ (students who actually enrolled in $p$):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{First Cohort} (main): $T_p$ = size of the first enrolled cohort observed for $p$.
    \item \textbf{Minimum Cohort} (robustness): $T_p$ = size of the \emph{smallest} enrolled cohort of $p$ over 2007--2016.
\end{itemize}
Always $T_p^{\text{Min}}\le T_p^{\text{First}}$: the Minimum Cohort definition is stricter and keeps only applicants who rank inside the
entering class of \emph{every} observed year, even the smallest.

\vspace{0.08cm}
\textbf{Definition.}
$\;\text{Inframarginal}_{ipt}=\mathbf 1\{Rank_{ipt}\le T_p\}$, applied to every ranked applicant, whether or not $i$ ends up enrolling in $p$.

\vspace{0.08cm}
\textbf{Example.} Program $p$ enrolled 60 students in its first observed year, then 80, 55, 70 (2007--2010).
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
 & $T_p$ & Rank 50 & Rank 58 & Rank 65 \\
\midrule
First Cohort & 60 & inframarginal & inframarginal & not \\
Minimum Cohort & 55 & inframarginal & not & not \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vspace{-0.1cm}
$T_p$ does not move with a given year's vacancies, so a capacity change in $t$ cannot change who counts as inframarginal.
\textbf{Caveat}: ranks count admitted plus wait-listed applicants and $T_p$ is based on enrollment, so ``inframarginal'' means
ranked inside a typical entering class, not a guarantee of admission.
\end{frame}

%--------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Step 2. The dependent variable depends on the definition}
\scriptsize
For program $p$ and year $t$, let $\mathcal I_{pt}(T_p)=\{i:\ i \text{ applied to } p \text{ in } t,\ Rank_{ipt}\le T_p\}$ be its inframarginal applicants
and $|\mathcal I_{pt}|$ their number. The outcome is
\[
\begin{aligned}
y_{pt}(T_p)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal I_{pt}(T_p)|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_{pt}(T_p)} \mathbf 1\{&i \text{ graduates within 8 years of } t\\
&\text{from the program where } i \text{ first enrolled}\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The sample behind each $y_{pt}$ is set by $T_p$.} First Cohort and Minimum Cohort contain different applicants,
    so they give different outcome values, different weights and different estimates. Each panel of the results table is a
    separate run with its own $y$.
    \item \textbf{Non-enrollees count as zeros.} An inframarginal applicant who never enrolls (or leaves higher education) counts as not graduating.
    $y$ therefore mixes take-up and completion. The population slide below restricts to enrollees as a check.
    \item \textbf{Which program?} Graduation is measured in the program where the student \emph{actually enrolled} first (MINEDUC, then DEMRE records).
    For most students this is $p$; an applicant ranked in $p$ who enrolls elsewhere counts as a graduate if they finish \emph{that} program.
    \item \textbf{Secondary outcomes} (same applicants): graduation from \emph{any university}, and from \emph{any higher-education institution}, within 8 years.
    These separate dropping out of the system from switching programs.
    \item \textbf{8 years} after the admission year, using titulados through 2024, so all cohorts 2007--2016 are fully observed.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%--------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Step 3. Treatment, instrument and model}
\scriptsize
\textbf{Treatment.} $N_{pt}$: total entering cohort of $p$ in year $t$ (all students enrolled in $p$, not only the inframarginal ones).
\textbf{Instrument.} $Z_{pt}$: total vacancies offered by $p$ in $t$. Neither depends on the inframarginal definition.

\vspace{0.1cm}
First differences within program, 2008--2016 (this removes any permanent program characteristic):
\[
\text{First stage:}\quad \Delta N_{pt} = \pi\,\Delta Z_{pt} + \alpha_t \;[+\;\gamma_{u(p)t}] + \nu_{pt}
\]
\[
\text{Second stage:}\quad \Delta y_{pt} = \beta\,\widehat{\Delta N}_{pt} + \alpha_t \;[+\;\gamma_{u(p)t}] + \varepsilon_{pt}
\]
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\beta$: change in the graduation rate of inframarginal applicants (pp, results $\times100$) per additional entering student that is induced by a change in vacancies.
    \item Two specifications: (1) year FE $\alpha_t$; (2) adds university $\times$ year FE $\gamma_{u(p)t}$, comparing programs of the same
    university in the same year. This absorbs university-wide shocks (budget, demand, policy) that move both vacancies and graduation.
    \item \textbf{Identifying assumption}: conditional on the FE, changes in vacancies affect inframarginal graduation only through the size of the cohort.
    The placebo on leads of $\Delta Z$ checks this.
    \item Weights: average number of inframarginal applicants of $p$ in $t$ and $t-1$ (same weights in first stage, reduced form and 2SLS). SE clustered by program.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Design I with a continuous-treatment DiD}
\scriptsize
\textbf{Why.} Design I's first stage and reduced form are TWFE regressions on a \emph{continuous} dose, $\Delta Z_{pt}$.
Callaway, Goodman-Bacon and Sant'Anna (2024) show that the TWFE slope:
\begin{itemize}
    \item is a weighted average of causal responses, with weights that need not match the dose distribution (and can be driven by unusual doses);
    \item mixes the \emph{level} effect of being treated with the \emph{slope} of the dose-response;
    \item under plain parallel trends it also picks up selection bias: comparing doses requires the stronger assumption that every dose group has the same potential trends.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.08cm}
\textbf{Parameters.} With dose $D$ and untreated group $D=0$:
\[
ATT(d)=E[\Delta Y\mid D=d]-E[\Delta Y\mid D=0],\qquad ACRT(d)=\frac{\partial\, ATT(d)}{\partial d}
\]
\[
ATT^o=E[ATT(D)\mid D>0],\qquad ACRT^o=E[ACRT(D)\mid D>0]
\]
\begin{itemize}
    \item $ATT(d)$ needs only parallel trends between dose $d$ and the untreated.
    \item $ACRT(d)$, the \emph{average causal response} (comparing doses), needs \textbf{strong parallel trends}: no selection on the gains from a given dose.
    \item \textbf{Capacity effects should scale with the dose}: congestion and peer composition depend on how many students enter.
    A level effect that does not grow with $d$ is hard to read as a capacity effect.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%--------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Continuous DiD: mapping to Design I}
\tiny
\begin{itemize}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
    \item Each program-year is a $2\times2$ comparison of $t-1$ and $t$. \textbf{Dose}: $D_{pt}=\Delta Z_{pt}$. \textbf{Untreated}: programs with $\Delta Z_{pt}=0$ (43\% of program-years).
    \item \textbf{Expansions} ($\Delta Z>0$) and \textbf{contractions} ($\Delta Z<0$, dose $|\Delta Z|$) are estimated separately, each against the same untreated programs. TWFE forces them to be symmetric.
    \item \textbf{Comparison programs}: untreated programs in the \emph{same year} (analog of FE (1)) or in the \emph{same university and year} (analog of FE (2); 96\% of program-years have one).
    \item \textbf{Estimation}: subtract the untreated mean of the cell, then regress on a cubic in the dose (B-spline of degree 3, no interior knots, the \texttt{contdid} default).
    $ACRT(d)$ is its derivative. Robustness: one interior knot at the median dose. Doses $\le 30$ seats ($\approx$P95).
    \item Same outcomes, sample and weights as Design I (First Cohort, inframarginal graduation within 8 years, weights avg.\ inframarginal cohort).
    \item \textbf{Per entrant}: $ACRT^o_{RF}/ACRT^o_{FS}$, the continuous-DiD analog of the 2SLS; compared with the TWFE ratio on the same sample.
    \item \textbf{Pre-trend}: same estimator on $\Delta y_{t-1}$. \textbf{Inference}: bootstrap clustered by program (499 reps), pointwise and uniform (sup-$t$) 95\% bands.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Design I: what the continuous DiD adds}
\footnotesize
\begin{enumerate}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{5pt}
    \item The \textbf{first stage survives}: each seat added or cut moves entry by about two-thirds of an entrant, all along the dose range.
    \item \textbf{No dose-response in inframarginal graduation.} $ACRT^o$ is close to zero per seat and per entrant, but imprecise:
    we cannot reject zero or the Design I estimate ($-0.033$ pp per entrant).
    \item Programs that \textbf{expand at all} see inframarginal graduation rise by about 1.8 pp, whatever the size of the expansion.
    An effect that does not scale with the number of entrants does not look like congestion or peer effects. More likely, programs that decide to expand
    differ from those that do not (a violation of parallel trends on the extensive margin). The pre-period $ATT^o$ is negative ($-0.7$ to $-0.8$, n.s.), consistent with mean reversion.
    \item The negative Design I estimate with university $\times$ year FE \textbf{comes from contractions}, which follow a decline in graduation (pre-period TWFE slope significant at 5--10\%).
    Together with the lead placebo, this weakens a causal reading of the $-0.033$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Design II: what happens when a selective program expands its seats?}
\scriptsize
\textbf{Research question}

When a selective program suddenly increases its regular-admission vacancies, what happens to
(a) its own first-year enrollment, (b) the composition of its entering cohort, and
(c) the outcomes of its inframarginal students?

\vspace{0.10cm}
\textbf{Approach}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A simple DiD built on Design I, but with a \emph{binary} treatment: a large, sudden own expansion.
    \item Design I uses every year-to-year change in vacancies, most of which are small and routine.
    Here, identification comes from discrete capacity decisions, and the event study makes pre-trends testable.
    \item The shock is the program's own decision, so it is not exogenous to its demand. Leads in the event study are the main diagnostic.
    \item Questions (a) and (b) are DiD effects of the shock. Question (c) uses the shock as an \emph{instrument} for the size of the entering cohort,
    with the inframarginal graduation outcome of Design I in the second stage (2SLS).
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.06cm}
\textbf{Sample}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Incumbent program $\times$ admission-year panel, 2007--2016 (programs with 2007--2009 entrants; SUA entrants excluded): 796 programs.
    \item \textbf{Selective programs}: top 20\% of 2007--2009 mean entrant PSU (predetermined; cutoff 633.6 points). 169 programs.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Design II: construction of variables and models}

\textbf{Treatment}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\text{shock}_{pt}=1$ if $\Delta\log V_{pt}$ is in the top 20\% of positive changes, $V_{p,t-2}$ is observed, $p$ was not shocked in $t-1$,

\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.08cm}

\[
\text{(1)}\quad y_{pt}=\beta\,\text{Treated}_{pt}+\mu_p+\lambda_t+\varepsilon_{pt}
\qquad\qquad
\text{(2)}\quad y_{pt}=\beta\,\text{Treated}_{pt}+\mu_p+\lambda_{u(p)t}+\varepsilon_{pt}
\]
{\tiny $\mu_p$: program FE. $\lambda_t$: year FE. $\lambda_{u(p)t}$: university $\times$ year FE (as in Design I, column (2)).}

\[
y_{pt}=\sum_{\ell\neq-1}b_\ell\,\mathbf 1\{t-g_p=\ell\}+\mu_p+\lambda_{u(p)t}+e_{pt},\qquad \ell\in\{\le-5,\dots,\ge4\}
\]

\begin{itemize}
    \item SE clustered by program.
    \item (D) Inframarginal outcomes, question (c): 2SLS with $\text{Treated}_{pt}$ as the instrument for $N_{pt}$ and the event study (B) of its reduced form. The 2SLS results close this design.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Design II (D): 2SLS for inframarginal graduation}
\scriptsize
\setlength{\abovedisplayskip}{2pt}
\setlength{\belowdisplayskip}{2pt}
\textbf{Idea.} The sudden expansion raises the size of the entering cohort (first stage = result (a)). We ask how much the graduation of the
students who would have been admitted anyway changes per additional entrant.

\vspace{0.06cm}
\textbf{Variables}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Endogenous regressor: $N_{pt}$, first-year enrollment of $p$ in $t$ (all entrants, Formulario D).
    \item Instrument: $\text{Treated}_{pt}=\mathbf 1\{t\ge g_p\}$, after the first own sudden expansion (same treatment as (A)).
    \item Outcome: $y_{pt}$, the graduation rate (\%) of the \emph{inframarginal applicants} of $p$ in $t$, \textbf{exactly as in Design I}:
    applicants with $Rank_{ipt}\le T_p$ (Step 1, First Cohort main, Minimum Cohort robustness), graduation from the program of first enrollment
    within 8 years, non-enrollees count as zeros (Step 2). Secondary: any university, any HE (8y), and program graduation at 10 years (cohorts $\le 2014$).
    $T_p$ is fixed per program, so the shock does not mechanically change who is inframarginal.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Model}, same FE sets (1) and (2) as the TWFE:
\[
N_{pt}=\pi\,\text{Treated}_{pt}+\mu_p+\lambda+\nu_{pt},
\qquad
y_{pt}=\beta\,\widehat N_{pt}+\mu_p+\lambda+\varepsilon_{pt},
\qquad \beta=\frac{\rho}{\pi}
\]
{\tiny $\lambda$: year FE in (1); university $\times$ year FE in (2). $\rho$: reduced form (effect of Treated on $y$).}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\beta$: pp of inframarginal graduation per additional entrant. Same units as Design I's 2SLS.
    \item Differences with Design I: binary, large and discrete instrument instead of every yearly $\Delta Z$; levels with program FE instead of first differences;
    unweighted. FS, RF and 2SLS use the same program-years (non-missing $y$). SE clustered by program.
    \item \textbf{Exclusion}: the shock also changes \emph{who} enters (entrant PSU falls, see composition). $\beta$ is the effect of an expansion of
    one entrant, size and composition together, not of class size alone.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Entry of private universities into the SUA}

\footnotesize

\textbf{Research question}

Did first-year enrollment in incumbent CRUCH programs change after eight
private universities entered the SUA in 2012, particularly in programs with
greater pre-existing exposure to these entrants?

\vspace{0.10cm}

\textbf{Sample}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Incumbent CRUCH program $\times$ admission-year panel, 2007--2016.
    \item Outcome: number of students entering the first year of program $p$
    in year $t$.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.06cm}

\textbf{Exposure design}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Markets are defined by pre-treatment region $\times$ academic field.
    \item Exposure measures the 2009--2011 enrollment presence of the future
    entrant universities in the incumbent program's market.
    \item Total exposure uses the entrant enrollment share; Triangular and
    Gaussian measures additionally weight entrants by PSU-score proximity.
    \item Exposure is fixed over time and interacted with the post-entry
    period, 2012--2016.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.05cm}

\scriptsize
The design compares pre/post-2012 enrollment changes across incumbent programs
with different levels of exposure to the future entrants.

\begin{frame}{Construction of the exposure measures}

\scriptsize
\setlength{\abovedisplayskip}{3pt}
\setlength{\belowdisplayskip}{3pt}

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]

\begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
\textbf{1. Pre-reform enrollment in market $m$}

\begin{center}
\resizebox{0.92\linewidth}{!}{
$\displaystyle
A_m
=
\sum_{\substack{q\in\mathcal E\\ m(q)=m}}
\overline{N}^{\mathrm{first}}_{q,2009\text{--}2011},
\qquad
T_m
=
\sum_{\substack{q\\ m(q)=m}}
\overline{N}^{\mathrm{first}}_{q,2009\text{--}2011}
$
}
\end{center}

\vspace{-0.1cm}

\begin{itemize}
\tiny
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}

\item $\mathcal E$ is the set of entrant programs and $m(q)$ is program
$q$'s pre-treatment region $\times$ field market.

\item $\overline{N}^{\mathrm{first}}_{q,2009\text{--}2011}$ is average
first-year enrollment: $A_m$ is entrant and $T_m$ total enrollment in $m$.

\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
\textbf{2. Total exposure}

\begin{center}
\resizebox{0.26\linewidth}{!}{
$\displaystyle
E_p
=
\frac{A_{m(p)}}{T_{m(p)}}
$
}
\end{center}

\vspace{-0.1cm}

\begin{itemize}
\tiny
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}

\item Entrant share of pre-reform first-year enrollment in the market of
incumbent program $p$.

\item Incumbents in the same region $\times$ field market share the
same $E_p$.

\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\end{columns}

\vspace{0.35cm}

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]

\begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
\textbf{3. Selectivity-weighted exposure}

\begin{center}
\resizebox{0.92\linewidth}{!}{
$\displaystyle
E_p^{k}
=
\frac{
\sum_{\substack{q\in\mathcal E\\ m(q)=m(p)}}
k\!\left(|S_p-S_q|\right)
\overline{N}^{\mathrm{first}}_{q,2009\text{--}2011}
}{
T_{m(p)}
}
$
}
\end{center}

\vspace{-0.1cm}

\begin{itemize}
\tiny
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}

\item $S_p$, $S_q$: pre-reform mean PSU scores of incumbent $p$ and
entrant $q$; $|S_p-S_q|$ is their selectivity distance.

\item $k\in\{\mathrm{Triangular},\mathrm{Gaussian}\}$ gives greater weight
to closer entrants, so $E_p^k$ varies within a market.

\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
\textbf{4. Kernel-weighted denominator}

\begin{center}
\resizebox{0.92\linewidth}{!}{
$\displaystyle
E_p^{k,\mathrm{KD}}
=
\frac{
\sum_{\substack{q\in\mathcal E\\ m(q)=m(p)}}
k\!\left(|S_p-S_q|\right)
\overline{N}^{\mathrm{first}}_{q,2009\text{--}2011}
}{
\sum_{\substack{\ell\\ m(\ell)=m(p)}}
k\!\left(|S_p-S_\ell|\right)
\overline{N}^{\mathrm{first}}_{\ell,2009\text{--}2011}
}
$
}
\end{center}

\vspace{-0.1cm}

\begin{itemize}
\tiny
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}

\item Same numerator as in 3; the denominator weights every program $\ell$
in the market (incumbents, including $p$, and entrants) by its
selectivity distance to $p$.

\item $E_p^{k,\mathrm{KD}}\in[0,1]$ is the entrant share of the enrollment
that is close to $p$ in selectivity.

\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\end{columns}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{First-stage specification}

\footnotesize

\[
N^{\mathrm{first}}_{pt}
=
\pi_k\left(E_p^k\times Post_t\right)
+\mu_p+\alpha_{f(p),t}
+\gamma_{r(p),t}+\nu_{pt}.
\]

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{3pt}

\item $p$ indexes incumbent programs and $t$ years.
$N^{\mathrm{first}}_{pt}$ is first-year enrollment of program $p$ in year $t$,
while $E_p^k$ is its pre-reform exposure and $Post_t=1$ in 2012--2016.

\item $f(p)$ denotes the pre-treatment field of program $p$ and $r(p)$ its
pre-treatment region. Accordingly, $\alpha_{f(p),t}$ are field $\times$ year
fixed effects and $\gamma_{r(p),t}$ are region $\times$ year fixed effects.

\item $\mu_p$ denotes program fixed effects, and $\pi_k$ is the post-2012
enrollment difference associated with a 10 percentage-point increase in
exposure measure $k$.

\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.08cm}
\textbf{Exposure is positive in only four regions}

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{3pt}

\item In the 2009--2011 analytical sample, entrant universities offered
regular undergraduate programs only in Metropolitana, Valparaíso,
Biobío and La Araucanía.

\item Thus, these are the only regions containing entrant programs and
positive entrant enrollment before integration into the SUA.

\item In every other region, the entrant-enrollment numerator is zero:
$A_m=0$, and therefore $E_p=A_{m(p)}/T_{m(p)}=0$.

\end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Cosine exposure}

\scriptsize
\setlength{\abovedisplayskip}{2pt}
\setlength{\belowdisplayskip}{2pt}

\tiny
Each program is represented by a vector describing the distribution of its
2011 first-year students. Each coordinate counts students with a particular
PSU profile and, depending on the definition, campus region and Broad field.
Programs are more similar when their students are distributed across the same
vector cells.

\vspace{0.06cm}

\textbf{1. Program vectors}

\begin{center}
\resizebox{0.82\textwidth}{!}{
\(
\displaystyle
\mathbf v_p^{PSU}
=
(v_{p,b})_{b\in\mathcal B},
\qquad
\mathbf v_p^{PSU\times R}
=
(v_{p,rb})_{\substack{r\in\mathcal R\\b\in\mathcal B}},
\qquad
\mathbf v_p^{PSU\times R\times F}
=
(v_{p,rfb})_{\substack{r\in\mathcal R,\;f\in\mathcal F\\b\in\mathcal B}}.
\)
}
\end{center}

\vspace{-0.10cm}

Here, \(b\) denotes a 25-point PSU bin, \(r\) campus region, and \(f\) Broad
field. Each vector cell contains the number of first-year students in that
combination. Entrant-program vectors are constructed analogously.

\vspace{0.05cm}

\textbf{2. Similarity between incumbent \(p\) and entrant \(j\)}

\begin{center}
\resizebox{0.56\textwidth}{!}{
\(
\displaystyle
\cos\theta^{d}_{j,p}
=
\frac{
\mathbf v_j^{d}\cdot\mathbf v_p^{d}
}{
\lVert\mathbf v_j^{d}\rVert
\lVert\mathbf v_p^{d}\rVert
},
\qquad
d\in
\left\{
PSU,\,
PSU\times R,\,
PSU\times R\times F
\right\}.
\)
}
\end{center}

\vspace{-0.10cm}

The cosine compares the \textbf{shape of the student distributions}, rather
than program size. Under interaction-cell definitions, similarity is zero
when programs do not share the indicated region or field.

\vspace{0.05cm}

\textbf{3. Additive similarity measures}

\begin{center}
\resizebox{0.80\textwidth}{!}{
\(
\displaystyle
\cos\theta^{PSU+R}_{j,p}
=
\frac{
\cos\theta^{PSU}_{j,p}
+
\mathbf 1\{r_j=r_p\}
}{2},
\qquad
\cos\theta^{PSU+R+F}_{j,p}
=
\frac{
\cos\theta^{PSU}_{j,p}
+
\mathbf 1\{r_j=r_p\}
+
\mathbf 1\{f_j=f_p\}
}{3}.
\)
}
\end{center}

\vspace{-0.10cm}

These measures give equal weight to PSU-profile similarity and coincidence
in campus region and, where applicable, Broad field.

\vspace{0.05cm}

\textbf{4. Exposure of incumbent program \(p\)}

\begin{center}
\resizebox{0.42\textwidth}{!}{
\(
\displaystyle
E_p^{d}
=
\sum_{j\in\mathcal J}
w_j\cos\theta^{d}_{j,p},
\qquad
w_j
=
\frac{
N^{\mathrm{first}}_{j,2011}
}{
\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal J}
N^{\mathrm{first}}_{\ell,2011}
}.
\)
}
\end{center}

\vspace{-0.10cm}

Exposure is higher when incumbent \(p\) resembles entrant programs that
account for a larger share of entrant enrollment.

\vspace{0.03cm}

\tiny
\textit{Notes:}
\(\mathcal J\) contains programs from the eight entrant universities.
Campus region is the university program's location in 2011. Student school
region and SES are not used.

\end{frame}

%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Design IV: what happens when a close competitor expands its seats?}
\scriptsize
\textbf{Research question}

When a close competitor $j$ of program $k$ suddenly increases its regular-admission vacancies, what happens to
(a) $k$'s first-year enrollment, (b) the composition of $k$'s entering cohort, and (c) the graduation of $k$'s inframarginal students?

\vspace{0.10cm}
\textbf{Approach}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A binary DiD, built like Design II: the treatment is an event at a \emph{competitor}, not at $k$ itself.
    \item Shock at $j$: the Design II shock ($\Delta\log V_{jt}$ in the top 20\% of positive changes, sudden; dated 2010--2016; 179 shocks).
    \item \textbf{Close competitor}: $j$ is in $k$'s market (campus region $\times$ broad field) and its 2007--2009 entrant PSU is within 50 points of $k$'s
    ($|S_k-S_j|<50$, the support of the triangular kernel).
    \item $k$ is treated from the first year a close competitor has a shock, and stays treated (absorbing).
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.06cm}
\textbf{Sample}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Incumbent program $\times$ admission-year panel, 2007--2016 (programs with 2007--2009 entrants; SUA entrants excluded): 796 programs.
    \item Selective vs selective: the 169 selective incumbents (top 20\% of 2007--2009 entrant PSU), treated only by shocks at selective close competitors.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\subsection{Inframarginal Design No. 5}
%=========================================================
% DESIGN V: INDIVIDUAL-LEVEL INFRAMARGINAL, APPLICATION-PORTFOLIO FE (issue #8)
% code/02_build/16, code/04_rdd/Inframarginals/36-38
%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Design V: Design I at the individual level}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Question.} Same as Design I: among students who would have been admitted anyway, do those who enter when the
cohort is larger graduate at lower rates?

\vspace{0.12cm}
\textbf{What changes relative to Design I.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Same variation}: changes over time in a program's vacancies $Z_{pt}$ around its historic mean, which move the size of the entering cohort $n_{pt}$.
    \item \textbf{Individual outcomes}: one row per inframarginal student, $Y_i=1$ if $i$ graduates within 8 years.
    We can control for PSU, NEM, sex and school type, which Design I cannot.
    \item \textbf{Application-portfolio fixed effects} $\phi_{ROL(i)}$: compare students who submitted the same list of programs (the ROL),
    which arguably holds fixed their preference for college and for this program.
    
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%--------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Design V: equations and sample}
\scriptsize
For inframarginal student $i$ in program $p$, admission year $t$ (the entering cohort of $p$ in $t$ is $n_{pt}$):
\[
\text{First stage:}\quad n_{pt} = \alpha_1 Z_{pt} + x_i'\alpha_2 + \psi_p + \psi_t + \psi_{ROL(i)} + \nu_{i}
\]
\[
\text{Second stage:}\quad Y_i = \beta\, n_{pt} + x_i'\beta_2 + \phi_p + \phi_t + \phi_{ROL(i)} + \varepsilon_{i}
\]
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sample}: inframarginal students as in Design I ($Rank_{ipt}\le T_p$, $T_p$ = first enrolled cohort). One row per student: the most preferred inframarginal
    application in the student's first inframarginal year (99\% of student-years have only one). \textbf{432,184 students, 1,307 programs, 8,218 program-years}, 2007--2016.
    \item \textbf{Outcome}: graduation within 8 years from the program where the student first enrolled (main); any university; any higher-education institution. 0/1 indicators: coefficients are changes in the probability of graduating.
    \item \textbf{Controls $x_i$}: PSU language+math, NEM, female, private-paid and municipal school (with missing indicators). \emph{No} peer composition: it is a mechanism.
    \item \textbf{Fixed effects}: program and year always (no specification without year FE). ROL FE under several definitions (next slide).
    \item \textbf{Inference}: SE clustered by program-year, the level at which $Z_{pt}$ and $n_{pt}$ vary.
    $\beta$: pp change in graduation per additional entering student induced by vacancies.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Decomposing weighted exposure}

\scriptsize

%---------------------------------------------------------
% ORIGINAL EXPOSURE AND EXACT DECOMPOSITION
%---------------------------------------------------------
\begin{center}
\resizebox{0.75\textwidth}{!}{
$
\displaystyle
\underbrace{
E_p^k
=
\frac{
\sum_{q\in\mathcal E_{m(p)}}
k\!\left(|S_p-S_q|\right)N_q
}{
\sum_{\ell\in m(p)}N_\ell
}
}_{\substack{
\text{Direct construction}\\
\text{of weighted exposure}
}}
\quad\equiv\quad
\begin{gathered}
E_p^k
=
\left[
\frac{
\sum_{q\in\mathcal E_{m(p)}}N_q
}{
\sum_{\ell\in m(p)}N_\ell
}
\right]
\left[
\frac{
\sum_{q\in\mathcal E_{m(p)}}
k\!\left(|S_p-S_q|\right)N_q
}{
\sum_{q\in\mathcal E_{m(p)}}N_q
}
\right]
\\[0.18cm]
E_p^k
=
\underbrace{
M_{m(p)}
}_{\substack{
\text{entrant}\\
\text{market share}
}}
\;\times\;
\underbrace{
Q_p^k
}_{\substack{
\text{similarity}\\
\text{with entrants}
}}
\end{gathered}
$
}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.05cm}

%---------------------------------------------------------
% COMPONENTS OF THE DECOMPOSITION
%---------------------------------------------------------

\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]

\begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}

\textbf{Entrant presence}

\tiny
\[
M_m
=
\frac{
\sum_{q\in\mathcal E_m}N_q
}{
\sum_{\ell\in m}N_\ell
}.
\]

\tiny
\(M_m\) is the entrant share of total market enrollment. It captures the
\textbf{size of entrant presence} and is common to all incumbent programs
in market \(m\).

\end{column}

\begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}

\textbf{Conditional similarity}

\tiny
\[
Q_p^k
=
\frac{
\sum_{q\in\mathcal E_m}
k\!\left(|S_p-S_q|\right)N_q
}{
\sum_{q\in\mathcal E_m}N_q
}.
\]

\tiny
\(Q_p^k\) is the entrant-enrollment-weighted PSU similarity between incumbent
\(p\) and the entrant programs in its market. It is higher when larger
entrants have PSU profiles closer to that of \(p\).
Conditional on entrant presence, it captures \textbf{competitive proximity}
rather than the overall size of entry.

\end{column}

\end{columns}

\vspace{0.06cm}

%---------------------------------------------------------
% FIRST STAGE USING Q
%---------------------------------------------------------

\textbf{Using similarity as the independent variable}

\tiny
\[
N^{\mathrm{first}}_{pt}
=
\beta_k
\left[
Q_p^k\times Post_t
\right]
+\mu_p+\alpha_{f(p),t}+\gamma_{r(p),t}
+\varepsilon_{pt},
\qquad M_{m(p)}>0.
\]

\tiny
The sample is restricted to markets with positive entrant presence.
Program fixed effects absorb time-invariant differences across incumbents,
while Broad-field \(\times\) year and pre-treatment region \(\times\) year
fixed effects absorb common field-specific and regional shocks over time.
The model therefore asks whether, among incumbents in exposed markets,
programs whose pre-treatment PSU profiles are more similar to the entrants
experience different post-2012 enrollment changes.
Unlike \(E_p^k\), which combines entrant market share and similarity,
\(Q_p^k\) isolates the \textbf{similarity component} of exposure.
The coefficient \(\beta_k\) measures the differential post-2012 enrollment
change associated with a 0.10 increase in entrant-enrollment-weighted PSU
similarity.

\end{frame}
